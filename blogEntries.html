Reflections on readings

  1. Theory: In Defense of the Poor Image by Hito Steyerl, 2011 / Inspiration: An Account of Peach Blossom Spring

      Reading Steyerl’s writing on the “poor image” took my thoughts in a variety of directions. I was moved by the ethos captured by the idea that the poor image in and of itself contains its history of transformation and the will for it to exist from the hands that have ushered it along the digital ether — across networks, systems, and varying formats — all the while relying on the fundamental aim to democratize access to the media it holds. In this way, the sheer existence of the poor image upholds an act of resistance, pushing against the exclusionary modalities that persist in hegemonic visual media production. At the same time, it’s dual-nature is unavoidable — the poor image’s engagement with the capitalist structures that it perhaps unintentionally usurps are also reinforced by it’s very persistence. Ambivalence is key to the concept of the "poor image."

      I asked myself at the beginning of the essay, is there such a thing as free distribution? Does engagement with the image itself come at a cost (beyond financial), especially when considering the apparatus used (i.e. the smartphone, the laptop, etc.). How has piracy changed as the consumer/commodity models of digital products, namely images in different forms, have also adapted to the ubiquity of online culture (e.g. the subscription model, streaming/VOD, ad-integration in social media, etc.)? The 2009 moment from which Steyerl theorizes represents significantly different contexts than 2026, although there is some room for the changes that have transpired in the writing of the essay.

      Nevertheless, the platforms wherein images predominantly circulate are different, and the topography of the digital landscape is also transformed. It was difficult for me to engage the “poor image” as an abstract yet pervasive entity without engaging the platforms where it may live today. Considerations for the tools and environments that are necessary to spawn poor images may also be important to interrogate, beyond the transgressive politics that they may inherently represent. Perhaps this can be evaded if the poor image is rendered into the physical realm rather than the digital, which in its increasing vastness has become increasingly monopolized by the privatization of digital communication. Steyerl articulates this: “Digital communication has also become one of the most contested markets – a zone that has long been subjected to an ongoing original accumulation and to massive (and, to a certain extent, successful) attempts at privatization.”

      Does the poor image which is “no longer anchored within a classical public sphere mediated and supported by the frame of the nation state or corporation, but floats on the surface of temporary and dubious data pools” hold as much value when it is placed in the oversaturated and monopolized digital image world we traverse today?

  2. Theory: The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction by Walter Benjamin, 1935 / Inspiration: Telegarden - Ken Goldberg

      Reading Benjamin’s essay, The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction, is like wading through a viscous sap of thoughts, waist deep and mildly electrified by their lucid grasp of the implications of cinema, even in its nascency. As the essay begins by adding to Marx’s remarkable prognostic ability, reading it is an activity that yields a similar feeling, especially in regards to Benjamin’s final forecast for art to take on an actively politicizing role in order to pushback on the fascistic takeover of politics into aesthetics. This is markedly palpable reading this essay now, a decade after reading it for the first time as an undergraduate.

      The intensity with which Benjamin captures the relationship of the moving image and the masses, elaborating on the essence of distraction in particular, has exploded in our engagement with visual culture at present. Duhamel’s radical critique quoted in the essay is deeply ironic, maybe even funny to reflect on today: “‘I can no longer think what I want to think. My thoughts have been replaced by moving images.’” The presuppositions that ground our entanglement with the medium of the moving image today seem technologically extraterrestrial to the early sound films of the 1930s, but I still believe in the subversive, artistic, and potent qualities of the moving image as a medium, and appreciate Benjamin’s words earlier in the essay when describing filmmaking potential: “The act of reaching for a lighter or a spoon is familiar routine, yet we hardly know what really goes on between hand and metal, not to mention how this fluctuates with our moods. Here the camera intervenes with the resources of its lowerings and liftings, its interruptions and isolations, it extensions and accelerations, its enlargements and reductions. The camera introduces us to unconscious optics as does psychoanalysis to unconscious impulses.”

      Despite the nearly 100 years that have passed since Benjamin wrote this essay, the human experience, especially in the fraught politics and plight of the masses, is still painfully resonant. The combination of capitalism and fascism is overwhelming, war is not called war, and art must be politicized further still.

  3. Theory: As We May Think by Vannevar Bush, 1945 / Inspiration: Man with a Movie Camera, 1929

      I am amazed by the human potential to both foresee and imagine future ingenuity, while also being unable to fully comprehend the full extent of its eventual manifestation. We have a tendency to build a future by harnessing the tools and circumstances with which we have become familiar, and concentrating on the most possible outcomes of what is currently cutting edge or in its prototype stages (for Bush, dry photography and microfilm).

      Reading Vannevar Bush’s essay from 1945 captured this feeling, and it was both funny and uneasy to encounter. This piece was written after the birth of my grandfather, with whom I am still able to sit and chat about the past. Moreover, seeing the ease with which he has eventually accepted and integrated technology like the smartphone into his life has been thought-provoking in this context. From having several questions many years ago, to eventually centralizing the technology (smartphone/tablet) in his daily life. At the end of the day, it contains all of his media and information needs, and channels the same spirit of the “Memex” device put forth in “As We May Think,” by providing my grandfather an interface to explore his questions and curiosities, sequester information, and access sources of news and media instantly.

      However, what is lost is perhaps the richness in the metaphysics that spawned Bush’s idea of “building trails” through “associative indexing.” Perhaps computer technology and all of the offspring in its nebula were originally conceived as ways to extend the human mind, our ability to harness and utilize information, to improve our world, and so on. However, the hopefulness in that origin is seemingly absent as corporate capitalism has matured to its present stage, and technological innovation is enraptured with amassing wealth and influencing government to support its endeavors in doing so.

      The tech-oligarchs have found bizarre kinship and effusive receptivity in our current fascistic government, and the technology that we are bestowed with today does not seem to be moving in the directions hoped by Bush, and are succumbing to the pitfall he foresaw: “The applications of science have built him a well-supplied house and are teaching him to live healthily therein. They have enabled him to throw masses of people against one another with cruel weapons. They may yet allow him truly to encompass the great record and to grow in the wisdom of race experience. He may perish in conflict before he learns to wield that record for his true good.”

      Wisdom and growth do not seem to be guiding principles in innovation today, and I am skeptical of Bush as well in his historical role in WWII with the military, and his significant contributions in developing the atomic bomb… Nevertheless, I am left thinking that it is indeed important to consider the broader negative implications of modern technological development, most notably in AI these past few years, and to take seriously the fears we hold. What may same like science-fiction thinking today may very well be an even lighter version of possible outcomes that we will witness in our lifetimes.

  4. Theory: Chapters 1 & 2 The Society of the Spectacle by Guy Debord, 1967 / Inspiration: Welcome to Jankspace, Babes by Daniel Felstead with Jenn Leung

      Is the world on it its head? Is the true a moment of the false? (Thesis 9) I feel myself chasing my tail trying to wrap my head around the spectacle as it stands today, thinking alongside the spectacle of which Debord writes in Society of the Spectacle. It is impossible not to concentrate on the sociality surrounding our technologically encumbered circumstances in the present day. Our trajectory along the axis from being —> having —> appearing (to have) has matured immensely, and virtuality seems superimposed onto reality. The spectacle is thriving, and Debord’s first two chapters outline the spiral that draws us deeper into it, and my thoughts feel like the swirling flush of water down the porcelain walls of a toilet bowl as I follow along.

      Images mediate the social relationships between people even more so today, and in fact seem to compose the main architecture of sociality as it functions. We are drenched in images. However, I am glad to remember a quote from Paula Rabinowitz’s essay, “Video Verite”: “Paradoxically, in the age of mechanical reproduction, which for Benjamin was primarily a visual age, words attain inordinate power; they become a necessary antidote to the fetishized image.” In this essay, Rabinowitz allows for some possibilities in the “captioning” that remains possible to the image, although this power can be and often is used nefariously.

      Returning to Debord, I am struck with his claim that “The origin of the spectacle is the loss of the unity of the world,” and that “The spectacle reunites the separate, but reunites it as separate.” Individuation has further consolidated, and communities are fracturing — it is finding ways to suture this fragmentation that motivate me. Although the economic system outlined in the essay has broken down even further, I try to look past Debord’s caveat in his quote, “When society discovers that it depends on the economy, the economy, in effect, depends on it. This subterranean power, which has grown to the point of seeming to be sovereign, has lost its power.” What power, if any, remains to be reclaimed?

  5. Theory: Constituents of a theory of the media by Enzensberger, 1970 / Inspiration: Arts and AI panel, 9/30 3-4:30pm; if you can't make select a participant to research: Minne Atairu, Annie Dorsen, Sydney Skybetter, Chris White

      Enzensberger’s essay, Constituents of a Theory of the Media, was thoroughly thought provoking, especially when elaborated into the potentiality of present-day technology. We have reached a visceral manifestation of a “communications medium,” well ahead of the “reciprocal action” offered by Brecht in regards to the radio, or to Enzensberger’s own calls for turning equipment like the television or film into receiver/transmitter assemblages. The reason for this, he suggests, “is consciously prevented for understandable reasons,” offering a critical dissection of the capitalist/bourgeois “fear of the masses” that cement these conditions.

      Moving towards the present, we can look at current technology as mediums of infinite communication. In fact, we can even communicate with the accumulation of large data sets that synthesize even more communications (AI), and we can speak with virtually any other person in the world if we so choose. Somehow, despite this, there still exist auxiliary strategies, born of the same socioeconomic structures, that inhibit the incredible possibilities available to us. In present media, we are perhaps afforded an unprecedented “means of production,” however there are several tactics at play that turn our labor back into serving the hegemons of capital, even we are engaging a resistance against them.

      Nevertheless, Enzensberger believes in the fundamental emancipatory potential of new media, and it has energized me to read and re-read his words. The depth of exploration he reaches will require multiple readings of this essay, although I am now able to see how “only a free socialist society will be able to make [the potential of the new media] fully productive.” In order to work towards this, it remains critically important for us to push at the threshold of the current technology that is available to us, and implement organizing activities towards collective structures that engage and produce new media. It seems that only through such combined action can true transformation be made possible, and the power of the masses may further assert itself.

Theory: "An Ironic Dream of a Common Language for Women in the Integrated Circuit," and "The Informatics of Domination" From A Cyborg Manifesto by Donna Haraway, 1985 / Inspiration: It's a Sound Piece or a Painting? Subtle Transfigurations of the Female Gutai Artist Atsuko Tanaka, Drawing After Electric Dress by Atsuko Tanaka; and a bit more if this work is of interest... Focus on... "Denkifuku" by Atsuko Tanaka

      Cyborgification has deepened and consolidated, boundaries continue to be blurred, and we continue to be “techno-digested” in our daily lives. Reading Donna Haraway weave a collective resistance in the formation of the cyborg has me re-thinking through the role of science and technology in shaping the very foundations of our ontological moment. The cyborg can show us where we are, and it can offer pathways of solidarity that move beyond the confines of identity that we continue to conjure for ourselves. In particular, I enjoyed the quote that traced the lineage that has spawned the cyborg as “the illegitimate offspring of militarism and patriarchal capitalism, not to mention state socialism,” and yet offered it an opportunity to become something utterly new, since “illegitimate offspring are often exceedingly unfaithful to their origins.”

      The door remains open as to how we deal with the consequences of our cyborg-natures. Nonetheless, there is tremendous pressure in the form of the “Informatics of Domination” that are at play. We have become deeply conditioned to think of human-beings as data sets, agglutinations of probabilistic and statistical figures that outline the entirety of our possibilities. When I think back to my undergraduate studies (B.A. in Integrative Biology), it seems obvious that “the translation of the world into a problem of coding” has been an ongoing trajectory of corporate capital as technology development, and the military industrial complex as science research. It seems then that the cyborg has been made forgetful by the very foundations that define its existence. As we become evermore reliant on technology in our daily life, the “fresh sources of power” introduced through that technology work against our memory of ourselves and our agency. It then becomes important to remember that technology is shaped by human social relations, and we are able to utilize technology and science ourselves to fight power through interventions (“analysis and political action”).

  7. Theory: Chapter 1 & 2 from The Electronic Disturbance by CAE, 1994 / Inspiration: Digital Resistance and Monkey-Wrenching by Grayson Earle; A Good Crisis, DIS with Moritz Shularick and Drew Zeiba

      The Critical Art Ensemble’s writing on virtualization and electronicalization began by capturing a potent set of key terms, such as program, pixels, image, representation, database, surface, and screen. These substrates of the electronic experience as posited in this 1994 work are deeply enmeshed in 2026. Where it seemed then that “Judging from the number of hours that the average person watches television, it seems that the public is electronically engaged,” the “autoexperience (a loss of the social)” of electronic engagement is perhaps even more pertinent today. We move with television-computer-communication conglomerations in our pockets. Where the private space of electronic engagement may have been a home with a television, our relationship to the technology of today allows us to erect a bubble of private space wherever we go. Even when out on the streets, one can retreat into an oblivion of “autoexperience of electronic media” at any moment. This has perhaps led to an even greater impotence of the public space as an environment in which cultural resistance may spontaneously generate, at least through simple presence. Our presence is perpetually mediated/muted; electronically.

      Nevertheless, a suggestion is offered to resist the global elite, today’s “nomadic power,” by using the technology that enshrines their power and dominance — hacking through the digital ether to unleash a panic that can disturb the “illusion of security” that we have come to experience. This is a powerful suggestion, especially when understood along the lines of some projects shared by Grayson Earle; Flood Net, Etoy War, Bail Bloc, and other examples that have manifested a form of digital resistance that I have spent much of this semester contemplating. These inspirations demonstrate that there are tactics “to expand the vocabulary of resistance” so that it can “include means of electronic disturbance.”

      The idea of “the gamble” is motivating and unnerving, when considered through a historical lens. Underneath the writing of this essay is an identification of how oppression has evolved, or rather was felt to be evolving through technology in particular. We often look back to the 90s as an example where there was possibly more liberation from the digi-tech shackles we experience gripping our physical and virtual limbs today. Does that make these two chapters clairvoyant, or is it our nostalgia that is inaccurate? There is an unsettling feeling that the critical writing which teases out the most threatening aspects of oppression in its own time, is always underestimating how the issues it captures in their nascency will only continue to proliferate and worsen over time. Is our approach to the necessary panic asymptotic? Forever approaching and nearing, waiting to cross the line.
