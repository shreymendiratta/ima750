Reflections on readings

  1. Theory: In Defense of the Poor Image by Hito Steyerl, 2011 / Inspiration: An Account of Peach Blossom Spring

      Reading Steyerl’s writing on the “poor image” took my thoughts in a variety of directions. I was moved by the ethos captured by the idea that the poor image in and of itself contains its history of transformation and the will for it to exist from the hands that have ushered it along the digital ether — across networks, systems, and varying formats — all the while relying on the fundamental aim to democratize access to the media it holds. In this way, the sheer existence of the poor image upholds an act of resistance, pushing against the exclusionary modalities that persist in hegemonic visual media production. At the same time, it’s dual-nature is unavoidable — the poor image’s engagement with the capitalist structures that it perhaps unintentionally usurps are also reinforced by it’s very persistence. Ambivalence is key to the concept of the "poor image."

      I asked myself at the beginning of the essay, is there such a thing as free distribution? Does engagement with the image itself come at a cost (beyond financial), especially when considering the apparatus used (i.e. the smartphone, the laptop, etc.). How has piracy changed as the consumer/commodity models of digital products, namely images in different forms, have also adapted to the ubiquity of online culture (e.g. the subscription model, streaming/VOD, ad-integration in social media, etc.)? The 2009 moment from which Steyerl theorizes represents significantly different contexts than 2026, although there is some room for the changes that have transpired in the writing of the essay.

      Nevertheless, the platforms wherein images predominantly circulate are different, and the topography of the digital landscape is also transformed. It was difficult for me to engage the “poor image” as an abstract yet pervasive entity without engaging the platforms where it may live today. Considerations for the tools and environments that are necessary to spawn poor images may also be important to interrogate, beyond the transgressive politics that they may inherently represent. Perhaps this can be evaded if the poor image is rendered into the physical realm rather than the digital, which in its increasing vastness has become increasingly monopolized by the privatization of digital communication. Steyerl articulates this: “Digital communication has also become one of the most contested markets – a zone that has long been subjected to an ongoing original accumulation and to massive (and, to a certain extent, successful) attempts at privatization.”

      Does the poor image which is “no longer anchored within a classical public sphere mediated and supported by the frame of the nation state or corporation, but floats on the surface of temporary and dubious data pools” hold as much value when it is placed in the oversaturated and monopolized digital image world we traverse today?

  2. Theory: The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction by Walter Benjamin, 1935 / Inspiration: Telegarden - Ken Goldberg

      Reading Benjamin’s essay, The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction, is like wading through a viscous sap of thoughts, waist deep and mildly electrified by their lucid grasp of the implications of cinema, even in its nascency. As the essay begins by adding to Marx’s remarkable prognostic ability, reading it is an activity that yields a similar feeling, especially in regards to Benjamin’s final forecast for art to take on an actively politicizing role in order to pushback on the fascistic takeover of politics into aesthetics. This is markedly palpable reading this essay now, a decade after reading it for the first time as an undergraduate.

      The intensity with which Benjamin captures the relationship of the moving image and the masses, elaborating on the essence of distraction in particular, has exploded in our engagement with visual culture at present. Duhamel’s radical critique quoted in the essay is deeply ironic, maybe even funny to reflect on today: “‘I can no longer think what I want to think. My thoughts have been replaced by moving images.’” The presuppositions that ground our entanglement with the medium of the moving image today seem technologically extraterrestrial to the early sound films of the 1930s, but I still believe in the subversive, artistic, and potent qualities of the moving image as a medium, and appreciate Benjamin’s words earlier in the essay when describing filmmaking potential: “The act of reaching for a lighter or a spoon is familiar routine, yet we hardly know what really goes on between hand and metal, not to mention how this fluctuates with our moods. Here the camera intervenes with the resources of its lowerings and liftings, its interruptions and isolations, it extensions and accelerations, its enlargements and reductions. The camera introduces us to unconscious optics as does psychoanalysis to unconscious impulses.”

      Despite the nearly 100 years that have passed since Benjamin wrote this essay, the human experience, especially in the fraught politics and plight of the masses, is still painfully resonant. The combination of capitalism and fascism is overwhelming, war is not called war, and art must be politicized further still.

  3. Theory: As We May Think by Vannevar Bush, 1945 / Inspiration: Man with a Movie Camera, 1929

      I am amazed by the human potential to both foresee and imagine future ingenuity, while also being unable to fully comprehend the full extent of its eventual manifestation. We have a tendency to build a future by harnessing the tools and circumstances with which we have become familiar, and concentrating on the most possible outcomes of what is currently cutting edge or in its prototype stages (for Bush, dry photography and microfilm).

      Reading Vannevar Bush’s essay from 1945 captured this feeling, and it was both funny and uneasy to encounter. This piece was written after the birth of my grandfather, with whom I am still able to sit and chat about the past. Moreover, seeing the ease with which he has eventually accepted and integrated technology like the smartphone into his life has been thought-provoking in this context. From having several questions many years ago, to eventually centralizing the technology (smartphone/tablet) in his daily life. At the end of the day, it contains all of his media and information needs, and channels the same spirit of the “Memex” device put forth in “As We May Think,” by providing my grandfather an interface to explore his questions and curiosities, sequester information, and access sources of news and media instantly.

      However, what is lost is perhaps the richness in the metaphysics that spawned Bush’s idea of “building trails” through “associative indexing.” Perhaps computer technology and all of the offspring in its nebula were originally conceived as ways to extend the human mind, our ability to harness and utilize information, to improve our world, and so on. However, the hopefulness in that origin is seemingly absent as corporate capitalism has matured to its present stage, and technological innovation is enraptured with amassing wealth and influencing government to support its endeavors in doing so.

      The tech-oligarchs have found bizarre kinship and effusive receptivity in our current fascistic government, and the technology that we are bestowed with today does not seem to be moving in the directions hoped by Bush, and are succumbing to the pitfall he foresaw: “The applications of science have built him a well-supplied house and are teaching him to live healthily therein. They have enabled him to throw masses of people against one another with cruel weapons. They may yet allow him truly to encompass the great record and to grow in the wisdom of race experience. He may perish in conflict before he learns to wield that record for his true good.”

      Wisdom and growth do not seem to be guiding principles in innovation today, and I am skeptical of Bush as well in his historical role in WWII with the military, and his significant contributions in developing the atomic bomb… Nevertheless, I am left thinking that it is indeed important to consider the broader negative implications of modern technological development, most notably in AI these past few years, and to take seriously the fears we hold. What may same like science-fiction thinking today may very well be an even lighter version of possible outcomes that we will witness in our lifetimes.
